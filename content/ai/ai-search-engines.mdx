An artificial intelligence search engine answers your question directly, in plain English, and shows the sources it used. You type a question, it reads pages across the web, and you get one written answer with links instead of ten links to dig through.

I use several of these every week, and the best one changes with the job. This guide covers what they are, the eight worth knowing, which to use when, and what to do if you want your own business to show up in them.

Prices and free limits verified November 2026. I re-check them every month.

## What is an AI search engine?

An AI search engine is a search tool that uses a large language model (the same kind of AI behind ChatGPT) to write an answer, not just list pages. It searches the web, reads the best results, and summarizes them with citations you can click.

### How is it different from Google?

Classic Google is a librarian who points at shelves. An AI search engine is a research assistant who goes to the shelves, reads the books, and comes back with a short briefing and the page numbers.

Google has blurred the line. It now has AI Overviews and AI Mode on top of regular results, so "Google vs AI search" is really "Google's blue links vs Google's AI answers." The practical difference: classic search is faster for finding one specific site, and AI search is better for questions that would otherwise take five tabs.

### How do AI search engines work?

In plain English, most follow the same three steps. First, they turn your question into one or more web searches. Second, they pull the top pages and read them. Third, the AI writes an answer using only what it found and attaches the sources. That is why they can answer things that happened this week, which a plain chatbot with no search turned on cannot.

## Which AI search engines are worth using?

Here are the eight I'd look at, grouped by what you're trying to do.

### Research with sources

**1. Perplexity**. Built around answers with citations from the start. I reach for it when I want sources on screen before I read a word of the answer. Pro is listed at about $20 a month in most sources I checked, with a limited free plan. Check Perplexity's pricing page for the current free limits, because they have changed more than once. Cites sources: yes.

**2. Google AI Mode and AI Overviews**. AI Overviews appear above regular results; AI Mode is Google's fuller AI search experience, and its answers come with links to explore. Google says it sometimes shows only web links when it is not confident in an AI answer. Free in the Google app and Search. The most advanced model in AI Mode is limited to Google AI Ultra subscribers with daily limits. Best for: people who already search in Google all day. Cites sources: yes.

**3. Microsoft Copilot Search (Bing)**. Microsoft's own page describes it as summarized answers with cited sources. Free to use. Reviewers note that follow-up answers cite sources less consistently than the first answer. Best for: Windows and Edge users. Cites sources: yes.

### Chat after the search

**4. ChatGPT search**. OpenAI says ChatGPT search is available to everyone in regions where ChatGPT is available, no signup required, and answers link to their sources. Best for: when you want to search, then keep talking: rewrite it, plan around it, turn it into a table. Cites sources: yes.

**5. Claude with web search**. Claude searches the web when it helps, and Anthropic's help page says every response includes citations. Free users can use web search too, but it counts toward usage limits that reset every five hours. Best for: research that turns into writing or work, especially if Claude is already your main assistant. Cites sources: yes.

### Privacy-first search

**6. Brave Search (Ask Brave)**. Brave says its AI answers sit right on the search results page and always cite their sources, with "no profiling." That is Brave's own marketing, not independent proof. Brave Search itself is free. Best for: privacy-minded people who want AI answers without a separate app. Cites sources: yes.

**7. Kagi**. A paid, ad-free search engine. As of November 2026 Kagi lists a free trial of 100 searches, then Starter at $5 a month (300 searches), Professional at $10 (unlimited), and Ultimate at $25 (unlimited). Prices exclude sales tax. Best for: people who are tired of ads and SEO spam and will pay to remove them. Cites sources: yes (check each Kagi feature, since AI features vary by plan).

### Multi-model option

**8. You.com**. A search engine that lets you switch between AI models and has a research mode for longer reports. I could not verify current consumer pricing, since You.com's public pricing page now covers only its developer API. Check the upgrade screen in the app for current plans. Best for: people who want to compare models inside one search box. Cites sources: yes.

## Which AI search engine should you use?

Pick by the job, not the brand.

- **You want sources you can verify fast:** Perplexity.
- **You already live in Google:** Google AI Mode.
- **You want to search, then work with the answer:** ChatGPT or Claude.
- **You hate ads and SEO spam:** Kagi.
- **You care about privacy and want it free:** Brave.
- **You use Windows and Edge:** Copilot Search.

If you only pick one to start, make it the one you'll actually open. A good tool you forget about loses to a decent one that's already in your browser. ## Do AI search engines cite their sources, and are they accurate?

Yes, they cite, but a citation is a pointer, not a guarantee. The AI can still misread a page or merge two sources. Click through for anything involving money, health, law, or a date. Google's own help page says to click the links in AI Mode to check information, and it is good advice for every engine on this list.

## How do AI search engines choose their sources?

They favor pages that are easy to read, directly answer the question, and look trustworthy. In practice that means a few things:

- **Clear answers near the top.** A page that states the answer in the first two sentences is easier to quote than one that buries it.
- **Pages the engine can actually reach.** If your site blocks crawlers or hides text inside images, the AI cannot read it.
- **Signals that other sites trust you.** Mentions, reviews, and links from credible sources make you a safer pick.
- **Fresh, specific facts.** Pricing, location, hours, and dates stated plainly beat vague marketing copy.

## How do you get your business shown in AI search engines?

You make your site easy for AI to read, quote, and trust. This is called answer engine optimization (AEO), and it builds on regular SEO rather than replacing it. Here are the steps I'd take, in order.

1. **Find the questions your customers actually ask.** Write down 10 to 20 real questions from DMs, emails, and sales calls. These are the prompts AI engines will answer.
2. **Answer each one on a page, answer first.** Put the direct answer in the first two sentences, then explain. One question per page or per heading.
3. **State the facts plainly.** Pricing, who it's for, location, and what you do. If a human has to guess, so does the AI.
4. **Make sure engines can crawl you.** Check that your site loads without logins, your pages are in your sitemap, and you are not blocking AI crawlers by accident.
5. **Add structured data where it fits.** FAQ and organization markup help machines read the page the way you meant it.
6. **Earn mentions off your own site.** Reviews, directories, podcasts, and partner pages that name you make you easier to recommend.
7. **Test and re-test.** Ask the engines above your own customer questions every month and see who gets cited. I use OpenSEO for AEO and SEO research and checking, and I tried an enterprise AI-visibility tracker first but found it too expensive and built for much bigger companies.
