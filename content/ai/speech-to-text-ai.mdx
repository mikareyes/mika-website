Speech to text AI is software that turns your voice into written words, and there are three different kinds: dictation (talk instead of type), transcription (turn a recording into text), and developer APIs (the engines other apps are built on). Most people searching this want the first one, and that is the one that changes your day.

I dictate most of what I write to AI. I use Wispr Flow, and it is about 3x faster than my keyboard. Below: which kind you need, my dictation setup, and where dictation breaks.

## What are the three kinds of speech to text AI?

Dictation, transcription, and developer APIs. Pick by the job, not the brand.

| Kind | What it does | Use it when | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dictation | Types what you say, live, into any app | You want to write emails, messages, and AI prompts by talking | Wispr Flow, Superwhisper, Apple Dictation, Windows voice typing |
| Transcription | Converts a recorded meeting, call, or file into a transcript and summary | You need notes, quotes, or captions from audio you already have | Otter, Rev, Fathom, Granola, MacWhisper |
| Developer APIs | Gives programmers the speech engine to build into their own apps | You are building a product that listens | OpenAI Whisper, Deepgram, AssemblyAI |

If you are not building an app, ignore the third row. If you have recordings piling up, jump to the transcription section. Everyone else, keep reading.

## How does speech to text AI work?

An AI model listens to audio, predicts the words, and then (in newer tools) a second model cleans up the result. Older dictation only guessed words. Modern tools also remove "um" and "uh," fix punctuation, and handle restarts like "send it Tuesday, no, Wednesday" by writing Wednesday.

That cleanup step is why AI dictation feels so different from the dictation built into your phone in 2015. You can ramble and still get a usable paragraph.

## Is speaking actually faster than typing?

Yes, for getting a first draft out. A Stanford-led study (Ruan et al.) measured speech at about 153 words per minute versus 52 on a phone keyboard. That test was on phones, in lab conditions, so treat it as a rough guide.

## How do I dictate prompts to Claude instead of typing them?

Open your dictation app, click into the Claude box, hold the shortcut, and talk like you are explaining the task to a coworker. That is it. The reason it works is not just speed.

When I type a prompt, I send a lazy one. "Make it better." Claude guesses wrong and I spend three more messages fixing it. When I speak, I naturally give context in one go: who it is for, what tone, what to keep, what to redo. One message instead of five. I covered this in [how to reduce Claude token use](/ai/reduce-claude-tokens-part-2), because fewer correction rounds means fewer tokens spent.

Three habits that help:

- **Say the goal first, then the constraints.** "I'm writing a reply to a customer who is upset about shipping. Keep it warm, under 100 words, no apology spiral."
- **Dictate the messy version.** Don't plan the paragraph. Talk, then let Claude shape it.
- **Read the text before you send.** Dictation is good, not perfect. Skim for names and numbers.

If you are new to prompting, my [vibe coding for beginners guide](/ai/how-to-vibe-code-for-beginners) shows the same talk-first approach with a coding agent.

> **Key insight:** Dictation is not mainly a typing shortcut. When you speak, you say more of what you actually mean, and AI does better work with more context.

## When does dictation fail?

Dictation fails in a few predictable places, and knowing them saves frustration.

- **Names, jargon, and acronyms.** Company names and technical terms get mangled until you add them to a custom dictionary, which most dedicated apps offer.
- **Noisy rooms.** Cafes and open offices hurt accuracy. A headset mic helps more than any setting.
- **Code and exact strings.** Dictating a URL or a command rarely goes well. Type those.
- **Thinking out loud in public.** Some people cannot dictate in an open office. That is a real limit, not a skill gap.
- **Phones.** I keep very few apps on my phone and do my real work on desktop, so my experience is desktop only. Wispr Flow lists iPhone and Android support, but I have not tested it there.

## Is speech to text AI private?

It depends on the tool, so check three things before dictating anything sensitive:

1. **Where does the audio go?** Cloud or on your device? Superwhisper's local models and OpenAI's open-source Whisper model can run on your own machine. Windows voice typing uses online speech recognition.
2. **Is your data used for training?** Wispr Flow says you can opt out of model training and delete your data any time. Apple asks whether to share audio to improve Siri and Dictation when you turn it on.
3. **Who can see transcripts?** For meeting tools, find out whether summaries are shared with your whole team or the people on the call.

For client work or anything confidential, use a local model or turn off data sharing, and tell the other people in a meeting that you are recording.
